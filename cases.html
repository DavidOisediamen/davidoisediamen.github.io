FICTIONAL CASE 1Human oversight

A reviewer who agrees 99% of the time.

An insurer runs AI triage on claims. Each reviewer clears 120 cases an hour and agrees with the model 99% of the time. On paper, a human is in the loop.

What the rules ask

  • EU AI ActOversight by people with the competence, training and authority to act (Articles 14 and 26(2)).
  • UKA right to human intervention in solely automated decisions with significant effect (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025).
  • NIST AI RMFMechanisms for oversight and intervention (MANAGE 2).
  • ISO/IEC 42001Roles and authorities assigned and communicated (Clause 5.3).

One oversight gate, tested against six criteria

  1. Authority
  2. Competence
  3. Information
  4. Time
  5. Independence
  6. A check on automation bias

The point. At 30 seconds a case, the time criterion fails first. Near-total agreement is the signature of a reviewer who can flag but not stop.

FICTIONAL CASE 2Two regimes

One CV screening tool, Amsterdam and London.

A recruiter uses one AI tool to rank candidates in both cities.

What the rules ask

  • EUEmployment is an Annex III domain, and ranking candidates profiles them, so the Article 6(3) exit is closed. The tool is high-risk, with obligations from 2 December 2027 under the Digital Omnibus.
  • UKNo AI statute. Where a significant decision is solely automated, UK GDPR Articles 22A to 22D, in force since 5 February 2026, require information, a chance to make representations, human intervention and a way to contest. The Equality Act 2010 applies with no AI carve-out.

One control set answers both

  1. A use-case classification form
  2. A user notice and explanation standard
  3. A human-in-the-loop decision gate
  4. A data quality and bias review
  5. An escalation and appeal route

The point. The EU tells you what to build and when. The UK tells you the outcome. Design controls to evidence outcomes, not activity, and one set answers both.

FICTIONAL CASE 3Maturity

Forty AI systems, and one zero that changed the score.

A mid-sized regulated organisation with about forty identified AI systems, twenty-eight of them vendor features rather than built systems, runs its first maturity assessment. Several criteria score 3.

What the assessment found

  • The averaged scoreData and Technical Foundations averages 1.6, which looks like steady progress.
  • The constrained scorePost-deployment monitoring scores 0: no system in production is observed. The constraint rule drops the dimension to 1.0.
  • The overall tierPartial. Pockets of real capability, but the estate is governed inconsistently.

Four actions, chosen by dependency, not ease

  1. Complete the inventory across built, bought and embedded AI, in 45 days
  2. Publish the intake route with service levels, in 30 days
  3. Set minimum monitoring by tier and apply it to the highest-tier systems already live, in 90 days
  4. Map obligations with a provider or deployer role per system, in 60 days

The point. The unconstrained mean hid the single most significant finding: every production system was unobserved. That is why Absent is scored separately from Partial.

FICTIONAL CASE 4Risk register

Accurate overall, failing for the smallest groups.

A candidate screening model is monitored on overall accuracy, reported monthly. Human reviewers check rejections, but they see each output, not the pattern across groups.

How the register scores it

  • Inherent riskLikelihood 4, impact 4: 16, Severe.
  • Current controlsOverall accuracy is effective for its purpose but the wrong measure. Review of rejections is only partly effective.
  • Residual riskLikelihood 3, impact 4: 12, High. That band needs acceptance above the AI Governance Owner.

Treatment: modify, with a named owner and a date

  1. Report precision, recall and specificity by relevant group
  2. Alert when the gap between groups exceeds 5 percentage points
  3. Suspend pending investigation if the gap passes the threshold
  4. Interim operation only with a monthly manual group-level review

The point. Aggregate accuracy can be true and still conceal systematically worse outcomes for the people least represented in the data.

Artefact 15: AI Risk Register
FICTIONAL CASE 5Ethics memo

A chatbot that went live against a deadline.

A customer service chatbot, fine-tuned on a large scraped dataset of internet conversations, has been live for three weeks and is now producing inappropriate and sometimes offensive responses. The delivery team had raised concerns about the dataset and were overruled.

How the memo reads it

  • LegalNo confirmed breach at discovery, but exposure under consumer protection and equality law, depending on content and who is affected.
  • EthicalThe system is harming the people it was built to help, and the harm is unlikely to fall evenly.
  • OptionsFilter outputs at the interface; take offline, fix the data and retrain; or withdraw the capability.

Determination: take it offline now

  1. Stopping ongoing harm outranks service continuity and the cost of the outage
  2. Document data provenance and filtering before any retrain
  3. Bias results reviewed by someone independent of the delivery team
  4. Daily output sampling for 30 days after re-release, by people who can suspend it

The point. Filtering at the interface treats the symptom and leaves the bias in the weights. The failure happened before deployment, not during it.
